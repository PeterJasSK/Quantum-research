1. EXACT ALGORITHM
=====================================================================================

1.1 Target and classical model
- Ising energy E(s) = - sum_{j>k} J_jk s_j s_k - sum_j h_j s_j, s in {1,-1}^n; mu(s) = exp(-E(s)/T)/Z.
  [P1: main Eq.(1), p.1]
- Spectral gap: delta = 1 - max_{lambda != 1}|lambda| of the 2^n x 2^n transition matrix P. Mixing-time bound
  (delta^-1 - 1) ln(1/(2 eps)) <= tau_eps <= delta^-1 ln(1/(eps min_s mu(s))). [P1: Eq.(9), p.3]

1.2 Full loop (Algorithm S1) [P1: SI Sec. II, Algorithm S1, SI p.2]
  1  s = initial spin configuration (often uniformly random)
  2  while not converged:
  3    Propose jump (quantum step 1)
  4    gamma = random.uniform(0.25, 0.6)
  5    t = random.uniform(2, 20)
  6    |psi> = exp[-i H(gamma) t] |s> on the quantum device
  7    s' = result of measuring |psi> in the computational basis
  8    Accept/reject (classical step 2)
  9    A = min(1, e^{[E(s)-E(s')]/T})
  10   if A >= random.uniform(0,1): s = s'
- State prep: the computational basis state |s> (s_j = +-1 = Z_j eigenvalue). [P1: p.2, "Step 1"]
- Measurement: every qubit in the Z basis, giving s'. [P1: p.2]
- Accept rule: Metropolis-Hastings Eq.(3) with Q(s|s')/Q(s'|s) = 1 because of the symmetry, so
  A = min(1, mu(s')/mu(s)). [P1: Eq.(3), p.2; Alg. S1 line 9; on hardware "set Q(s|s')/Q(s'|s) = 1 in the
  acceptance probability", p.5]. Gibbs/Glauber acceptance A_G (Eq. S8) also tested: "little impact". [P1: SI
  Sec. III C3, SI p.8]

1.3 Hamiltonian -- form verified
- H = (1 - gamma) alpha H_prob + gamma H_mix   [P1: Eq.(5), p.2]
- H_prob = - sum_{j>k} J_jk Z_j Z_k - sum_j h_j Z_j = sum_s E(s)|s><s|   [P1: Eq.(6), p.3]
- H_mix = sum_{j=1}^n X_j   [P1: Eq.(7), p.3]
- alpha = ||H_mix||_F / ||H_prob||_F = sqrt(n) / sqrt( sum_{j>k} J_jk^2 + sum_j h_j^2 )   [P1: Eq.(8), p.3]
  (||M||_F = tr(M^dag M)^{1/2}, the Frobenius norm.) Quote: "which -- crucially -- can be computed in O(n^2) time."
  Purpose: "so that both terms of H share a common scale regardless of {J_jk, h_j}". [P1: p.3]
- gamma in [0,1]. alpha multiplies ONLY H_prob, and H_prob is NOT divided by T. So the quantum proposal Q(s'|s)
  does not depend on T. [P1: Fig. 5 caption, "none depend on T or on the choice of acceptance probability"]
- SI Sec. III B shows how alpha scales: <alpha> ~ sqrt(2)/(sigma sqrt(n)) for fully connected instances (Eq. S6)
  and ~ 1/(sigma sqrt 2) for 1D chains (Eq. S7). So "the 1/sqrt(n) scaling of the original S-K model arises
  naturally in the quantum part of our algorithm". [P1: SI p.5]
- More general allowed H_mix: "arbitrary weighted sums and products of X_j and Y_jY_k terms" (keeps H real
  symmetric). [P1: p.3]
- Not adiabatic: "Note that we do not invoke adiabatic quantum evolution in any way ... the relative weights of
  H_prob and H_mix are held fixed throughout each quantum step." [P1: p.3]

1.4 How gamma and t are drawn
- In the algorithm as stated: gamma ~ U[0.25, 0.6], t ~ U[2, 20], drawn fresh each MCMC iteration.
  [P1: Alg. S1 lines 4-5; SI p.2; main p.3 "picking gamma and t uniformly at random in each iteration"]
- How they were chosen: "We settled on these ad hoc distributions through trial and error on a small number of
  fully-connected random model instances, and found them to be robust across a wide range of instances for
  various n and model connectivities." [P1: SI p.2]
- Numerics actually used a discrete gamma: 20 midpoints of [0.25,0.6], Delta-gamma = 0.0175 (Eq. S3); the t
  integral was done analytically as a channel average (Eq. S2). [P1: SI Sec. III A, SI p.4]
- Hardware used gamma in Gamma = 10 midpoints, Delta-gamma = 0.035 (Eq. S17), and t in
  T = {r*Dt | 2 <= r <= 25} = {1.6, 2.4, 3.2, ..., 19.2, 20} with Dt = 0.8 (Eq. S18). That is 10 x 24 = 240
  (gamma,t) settings. [P1: SI Sec. IV A3, SI pp.19-20]
- t is not scaled with n: "t did not depend on the problem size n in our implementation, although in some
  settings it should grow with n if the qubits are all to remain within each others' light cones." [P1: p.7]

1.5 Trotterization on hardware [P1: SI Sec. IV A1, SI pp.15-17; main p.5]
- Split H = H1 + H2:
  H1 = -(1-gamma) alpha sum_j h_j Z_j + gamma sum_j X_j   (1-body)   [Eq. S13]
  H2 = -(1-gamma) alpha sum_{j>k} J_jk Z_j Z_k            (2-body)   [Eq. S14]
- Order: 2nd-order product formula V = (e^{-iH2 Dt/2} e^{-iH1 Dt} e^{-iH2 Dt/2})^r, Dt = t/r. [Eq. S15]
- The first and last half-steps of e^{-iH2} are diagonal, so they do not change computational-basis
  statistics and are dropped. What was run is
  V~ = e^{-iH1 Dt} (e^{-iH2 Dt} e^{-iH1 Dt})^{r-1} [Eq. S16]. This is "a 2nd-order r-step PF (or equivalently,
  a 1st-order r-step PF [21]) using only r-1 applications of the entangling Trotter step". [SI p.16]
- Step size: "We used a Trotter timestep of Dt = 0.8 for all experiments in this work", picked as a good
  trade-off between Trotter and gate error, independent of t, gamma and instance. [SI p.16]
- Gates: e^{-iH1 Dt} = parallel single-qubit exp[-i(a_j X_j + b_j Z_j)], a_j = gamma Dt, b_j = -(1-gamma) alpha
  h_j Dt, done as Qiskit "U" gates (two X90 pulses + virtual Z). e^{-iH2 Dt} = R_ZZ(theta_jk),
  theta_jk = -2 J_jk (1-gamma) alpha Dt, in two layers of parallel R_ZZ on a 1D chain (Fig. S8).
  R_ZZ is built as H.R_ZX.H with pulse-efficient (scaled cross-resonance) R_ZX, angle folded into
  [-pi/2, pi/2] (Figs. S9, S10). So each R_ZZ costs "a fraction of a CNOT rather than two CNOTs". [SI pp.16-17]
- Depth: "a randomly-compiled second-order Trotter-Suzuki product formula ... with up to 48 layers of
  pulse-efficient 2-qubit gates acting on up to 5 pairs of qubits in parallel". [P1: main p.5]
  Consistent with r_max = 25: 24 entangling steps x 2 layers = 48. [INF]
- Gate counts: no total gate count is printed. [not in P1]. [INF] For n=10 on a 1D chain each entangling step has
  9 R_ZZ (5+4), so the deepest circuit (r=25) has 24 x 9 = 216 pulse-efficient R_ZX gates, plus 25 layers of
  1-qubit U gates. The shallowest (t=1.6, r=2) has 1 entangling step (9 R_ZX).
- 1D only on hardware: "we implemented our algorithm exclusively for 1D problem instances in order to avoid SWAP
  gates". [SI p.16; main p.5]
- Noise tailoring, used in all experiments: SPAM twirling (random key c, prepare |s XOR c>, flip signs of
  J,h, XOR the output; Fig. S11) and gate twirling (random Paulis around each R_ZX; Fig. S12). [SI pp.17-19]
- Experiment layout: H^{(x)n} on |0>^n, measure to get a random s, then the Trotter circuit, then measure s'
  (Fig. S13). Each of the 240 (gamma,t) settings x N_twirl random twirls was run for N_shots shots.
  [SI pp.19-20]
- NOT run online: "We did not experimentally implement our MCMC algorithm 'online' by alternating between
  quantum and classical steps in real time. Doing so would have been impractically slow due to limitations in
  the classical infrastructure around our quantum processor." Chains were built offline from a cache of
  measured transitions ("Markov chain subsampling", sampling without replacement). [SI Sec. IV B1, pp.20-21]
- The hardware "delta" is inferred: estimate Q-hat(j|k) from counts (Eq. S20), build P-hat with the exact
  classical M-H acceptance (Eqs. S21-S22), then diagonalize. [SI Sec. IV B, p.20]. [INF] This needs the full
  2^n x 2^n matrix, so it is a small-n validation method, not a scalable one (the authors say this too, sec. 7).

2. SYMMETRY / DETAILED-BALANCE ARGUMENT
=====================================================================================

2.1 What is required [P1: Eq.(4), p.2]
- Constraint: |<s'|U|s>| = |<s|U|s'>| for all s, s'. Then Q(s'|s) = |<s'|U|s>|^2 = Q(s|s'), and the
  intractable Q ratio in Eq.(3) is 1. Quote: "While computing Q(s'|s) and Q(s|s') may take exponential (in n)
  time in general, there is no need to do so: Eq. (3) depends only on their ratio, which equals 1 since
  Q(s'|s) = |<s'|U|s>|^2 = Q(s|s') due to (4)." [p.2]
- The quantum step is only the proposal. Detailed balance comes from the classical M-H acceptance; convergence
  to mu also needs irreducibility and aperiodicity. [p.2; SI Sec. II A, p.3]

2.2 Why U satisfies it [P1: p.3; SI Sec. V C, Eqs. S40-S41, SI p.37]
- Real symmetric H gives U = U^T: "These produce a symmetric H and therefore U = U^T (although U != U^dag
  generically), thus satisfying condition (4)." [p.3]
- SI version: with <k|H_mix|j> real, "H is real and symmetric (not just Hermitian), so it has real eigenvalues
  ... and a set of real orthonormal eigenvectors", so U^T = sum e^{-i lambda t}|l><l|^T = U (Eq. S41). [SI p.37]
- So the condition is: real H in the computational basis (= H^T), equivalently built from Z, X, YY-type real
  terms. A Y_j single term would break it. [INF from the stated allowed set "X_j and Y_jY_k terms"]
- Time-dependent H: a sufficient condition is H(t)^T = H(t) and H(t) = H(tau - t) (palindromic schedule),
  proved in SI Eqs. S42-S46. Used to justify reverse annealing (Eq. S47) as a valid proposal. [SI pp.37-38]
- Random mixtures: "instead of applying the same U in every iteration ... one could equally well pick U at random
  in each iteration from an ensemble {U1, U2, ...} of unitaries, each satisfying condition (4)." [p.3]
  As stated, P1 asks that EACH member be symmetric. [INF] Mathematically only the averaged Q has to be symmetric.
  P1 points at this only in the noise context ("symmetry on average", p.7) and does not state it as a theorem.
- Irreducibility/aperiodicity: sufficient if Q(s'|s) > 0 for all s,s'. Formally guaranteed by mixing in a
  uniform proposal with probability eps, but "In practice, however, we found no need for this formality. Absent
  some special symmetry, no s -> s' quantum transition should be completely forbidden. Considering also
  experimental noise, Q(s'|s) > 0 for all s and s' is all-but formally assured". [SI p.3]

2.3 Does it survive Trotterization?
- P1 does not explicitly prove that the Trotter circuit is symmetric. [not in P1 as an explicit statement]
- [INF] It does survive. Every factor is a transpose-symmetric matrix: e^{-iH1 Dt} is a product of single-qubit
  exp[-i(aX+bZ)] (X, Z are real symmetric), and e^{-iH2 Dt} is diagonal. The 2nd-order palindrome V = ABA gives
  V^T = A^T B^T A^T = V, and V^r is symmetric too. The implemented V~ = e^{-iH1}(e^{-iH2}e^{-iH1})^{r-1} is also a
  palindrome in the factors, so V~^T = V~. Dropping the diagonal end layers only changes phases. A NON-palindromic
  ordering of non-commuting real factors (e.g. ABC with A != C) is generally not transpose-symmetric. That would
  break Eq.(4) unless it is fixed by randomizing the ordering.
- Twirled circuits: the SPAM-twirled circuit "do[es] not implement the same unitary" but gives the same
  transition probabilities after XOR post-processing. [SI p.17-18]

2.4 Noise and correctness -- what the authors say
- "(We emphasize that quantum errors do not necessarily bias our MCMC algorithm, i.e., cause it to converge to the
  wrong distribution. Rather, it is asymmetry in the quantum errors that can have this effect.)" [SI p.17]
- "We found that implementing bare Trotter circuits like that in Fig. S8 clearly breaks this symmetry, which could
  bias our algorithm. We attribute this to biased errors in the two-qubit gates and the readout". Fix: SPAM +
  gate twirling. [SI p.17]
- Symmetry was tested with Bowker's test on IID subsamples (200 subsamples, 1% threshold) [SI Sec. IV B2, pp.21-22]:
    n=10: <p_sym> traditional 36.7%, modified 25.1% [Fig. S15]
    n=9 : 12.7% / 12.7% [Fig. S20]
    n=8 : 41.4% / 41.4% [Fig. S27]
  All are above 1%, so the data are consistent with symmetry.
- Honesty caveat: "Note that exact Q(s|s') = Q(s'|s) symmetry is impossible in a complex engineered system like
  a quantum computer ... we do not claim that any of our experiments realized a Q that was exactly symmetric,
  only that it was symmetric enough for practical purposes." A wrong ratio "may converge to a slightly different
  distribution than intended [41], which may slightly bias thermal average estimates ... We could not resolve any
  such biases in our experiments." Rule of thumb: "if the (potential) asymmetry in Q is too small to detect from
  the data at hand, it is probably too small to make much difference in your MCMC results ... long Markov chains
  are more sensitive to such asymmetry." [SI p.22]
- Main-text robustness claim: "if an error occurs in the quantum processor while a jump is being proposed, the
  proposal will be accepted/rejected as usual, and the Markov chain can still converge to the target distribution
  provided such errors do not break the Q(s'|s) = Q(s|s') symmetry on average [11]. Rather than produce the wrong
  result, we found that such errors merely slow the convergence at low T by making our algorithm more classical.
  Indeed, in the limit of fully depolarizing noise, our algorithm reduces to MCMC with a uniform proposal." [p.7]
- [INF] No quantitative bound links measured asymmetry to bias in mu. The only check is the Bowker test plus
  magnetization traces that converge "with no discernible bias" (p.6).

3. INSTANCE FAMILIES, n, COUNTS, TEMPERATURES
=====================================================================================

3.1 Simulation ensembles (exact, noiseless)
- Fully connected "SK-like": J_jk, h_j IID N(0,1), all couplings nonzero (almost surely). It differs from SK
  because (i) it adds random fields h_j and (ii) it uses variance 1 rather than 1/n (no 1/sqrt(n)).
  "it would be trivial to introduce this 1/sqrt(n) scaling into our results by simply rescaling the temperature
  as T -> T/sqrt(n)." [P1: p.4; SI Sec. III B, SI pp.4-5]
- 1D open chain: J_{j+1,j}, h_j ~ N(0,1), all other J = 0. [SI Sec. III C1, SI p.5; Fig. S1]
- n = 3..10, 500 random instances per n, for each family. [p.4 "500 random spin glass instances"; Fig. 2,
  Fig. S1 captions]. The same instances were reused for Figs. S2-S7.
- Mismatched-quantum control: same distribution, but the quantum step uses a different random (J~, h~). [p.4]
- Simulations cover only n <= 10. No fully connected simulation at larger n. [not in P1]

3.2 Hardware instances (illustrative, hand-picked)
- Three 1D instances, n = 10 (main text), n = 9 and n = 8 (SI). Coefficients are printed in Eqs. S26, S29, S30.
- Selection: "We generated random model instances and picked illustrative ones whose lowest-E configurations
  include several near-degenerate local minima" [p.5]. For n=10: "the six lowest-E configurations are all local
  minima, and the two lowest have an energy difference of just |DE| = 0.05". [p.5]
- One instance per n on hardware. No ensemble on hardware. [P1: SI Sec. IV C]

3.3 Temperatures
- delta vs T sweeps go continuously over T = 0, 10^-3 ... 10^3, and T = infinity (Figs. 2a, 3, S1a, S2, S4, S6,
  S16, S17, S21, S22, S28, S29).
- Scaling fits are at T = 10^-2, 10^-1, 0.5, 1, 5, 10, 10^2 (Fig. 2b at T=1; Fig. S3; Tables S1-S3).
- Magnetization/trajectory experiments use only T = 0.1 (Figs. 4, S23, S30).
- How T = 0.1 was chosen: "This T is therefore sufficiently high that sampling from mu is not simply an
  optimization problem (where <m>_mu depends overwhelmingly on the ground configuration), but sufficiently low
  that <m>_mu is mostly determined by a few low-E configurations out of 2^n = 1024." [p.5]
  Boltzmann weights at T=0.1:
    n=10: ground, 1st, 2nd, 3rd excited ~ 43%, 26%, 19%, 12%; <m>_mu ~ 0.15 [p.5]
    n=9 : ~ 37%, 36%, 27%; <m>_mu ~ 0.35 [SI p.27]
    n=8 : ~ 39%, 32%, 26%; <m>_mu ~ -0.16 [SI pp.31-32]
- Stated focus: "For small but non-zero T, sampling from mu requires finding several of the lowest-energy
  configurations, which may be far apart, not just the ground configuration(s). This hard regime will be our main
  focus." [p.1]

  imperfections." [p.6]

4.3 Cluster-algorithm comparison (illustrative 1D instances only)
- Swendsen-Wang (ghost-spin and accept/reject variants), Wolff (both variants), and Houdayer (R=2 replicas,
  single T) on the n=10, 9, 8 hardware instances [Figs. S16, S21, S28; SI Sec. III D]. "These are substantially
  more complicated than the local and uniform proposals, both conceptually and practically, but offer almost no
  delta enhancement in this setting compared to the simpler classical alternatives, so we do not focus on them
  here." [p.5]. "Despite their relative complexity, these cluster algorithms perform comparably to M-H with a
  local or uniform proposal, and converge especially slowly in the low-T regime of interest." [SI p.15]
- Lazy chains and Gibbs acceptance were checked to rule out a trivial eigenvalue-near-minus-1 artifact: "it is
  not ... the only setting where lazy chains enhance delta is for the local proposal at high T." [SI p.11;
  Figs. S6, S7, S17, S22, S29]

6. TEMPERATURE DEPENDENCE -- EVERYTHING

- YES, there are explicit plots of advantage vs T: Fig. 2a (n=10 average), Fig. S2 (n=4..9), Fig. S1a (1D), Fig.
  S4/S6 (Gibbs/lazy), Fig. 3 and S16/S21/S28 (hardware instances, delta vs T from 0 to infinity), and Tables

- Key quotes:
  * "At high T, where mu is nearly uniform, the uniform proposal produces a fast-converging Markov chain with delta

    lower T. This slow-down is much less pronounced for our quantum algorithm, which gives a substantially better
    delta on average than either classical alternative for T <~ 1." [p.4]
  * Fig. 2a caption: "The slow-down of each strategy at low T. For the local proposal strategy, delta -> 0 also at
    high T because an eigenvalue of its transition matrix approaches -1. This artifact can be easily be remedied by
    using a lazy chain or the Gibbs sampler acceptance probability; the same is not true at low T, however [11]."
  * "These values depend on T, but their ratios suggest a roughly cubic/quartic enhancement at low temperatures
    regardless of the exact T [11]." [p.4]
  * SI: "Each panel of Fig. S2 ... show a growing separation in delta with increasing n at low T between our
    quantum algorithm and the purely classical Markov chains. Similarly, the panels of Fig. S3 show the onset of an
    apparent scaling advantage of delta with n ... which gets increasingly pronounced as T decreases." [SI p.6]
  * Hardware: "At high T, the experimental delta is larger than the theoretical value ..., which we attribute to
    noise in the quantum processor mimicking the uniform proposal to a degree." [p.5]
  * Motivation: Eq.(3)'s ratio "is exponentially small in DE/T ... Such proposals are frequently rejected ... This
    makes it difficult for Markov chains to explore rugged energy landscapes at low T". [p.2]
- What the curves show (~ read from Fig. 2a/S2):
  * Low T (T <~ 0.1): every strategy's delta reaches a T-independent floor. Quantum's floor is about 1-2 decades
    above uniform's. Local goes toward 0.
  * Crossover T ~ 1: at n=10 the quantum curve rises to its plateau, while uniform/local rise much more steeply.
  * High T (T >~ few): uniform delta -> 1, far above quantum's plateau (~0.3 at n=10). Quantum is WORSE than
    uniform in absolute delta at high T, at all n shown. At T = 10^2 no exponential fits are reasonable (Table S1
    "-"); the quantum and uniform deltas are flat in n (Fig. S3, T=10^2 panel).
  * [INF] The quantum high-T plateau is below 1 because Q(s|s) (no-move) and the shape of Q are T-independent.
    The proposal is not uniform, so even at mu ~ uniform the quantum chain mixes more slowly than uniform proposals.
- Phase transition / critical temperature: P1 says NOTHING about a spin-glass transition, a critical T, or
  behaviour "near" any transition. [not in P1]. [INF] At n <= 10 there is no sharp transition. In P1's units
  (J variance 1, no 1/sqrt(n)), the standard SK T_c = 1 would map to T ~ sqrt(n) (~3.2 at n=10) via the stated
  T -> T/sqrt(n) rescaling, ignoring the random fields, which in the infinite-size SK model remove a sharp
  transition (AT-line physics). The paper's crossover near T ~ 1 has not been compared to any such scale.
- How T was chosen: there is no principled choice. Sweeps cover all T; T=0.1 for trajectories is justified as
  "not pure optimization, but few low-E states dominate" (quote in 3.3). [p.5]

7. LIMITATIONS, CAVEATS, FUTURE DIRECTIONS, BASELINES
=====================================================================================
7.1 Stated limitations / caveats (quotes)
- Partly heuristic: "In this sense our algorithm is partially heuristic, like most classical MCMC algorithms: the
  eventual result is theoretically guaranteed, while fast convergence is established empirically." [p.6]
- Scaling with n unknown under noise: "Our simulations suggest that the quantum speedup increases with the
  problem size n. However, we also expect the quantum noise to increase with n, in the absence of error
  correction, as the number of potential errors grows. The combined effect of these competing factors at larger n
  is currently unknown." [p.7]
- Fault-tolerant outlook is conditional: "the cubic/quartic speedup we observed, should it persist at larger n,
  might give a quantum advantage on a modest fault-tolerant quantum computer despite the error correction overhead
  [34, 47]." [p.7]
- delta is not measurable at scale: "a Markov chain's absolute spectral gap is a broad and unambiguous figure of
  merit, but it is not feasible to measure for large instances ... a more fruitful approach may be to focus
  directly on how well our algorithm performs in various applications, such as in simulated annealing ..., for
  estimating thermal averages ..., or for training and sampling from (classical) Boltzmann machines". [p.7]
- Computing delta is not the same as running MCMC: "(Note that computing delta is different -- and much more
  demanding -- than simply running MCMC.)" [p.3]
- Exact symmetry is impossible; there is a possible small bias (SI p.22, quoted in 2.4).
- The bare circuits broke symmetry; twirling is required (SI p.17).
- Offline, not real-time; "impractically slow" with the current classical control stack (SI p.20).
- gamma, t not optimized; "ad hoc distributions through trial and error" (SI p.2).
- Hardware is 1D only (no SWAPs); the scaling claim is for fully connected instances in simulation.
- Cluster algorithms are compared only on illustrative instances, and "not designed for spin glasses" (SI p.13).

7.2 Cost per step / wall-clock
- No wall-clock comparison, no time-per-iteration, no cost-per-step accounting (quantum circuit time + latency
  vs a classical move). [not in P1]
- The only cost statements: alpha costs O(n^2) (p.3); the M-H ratio costs O(n^2) (p.2); circuit depth up to 48
  2-qubit layers (p.5); t is independent of n in their runs (p.7); the experiment structure trades fewer circuit
  loads for more shots (SI p.19).
- [INF] All speedup claims are in iterations (spectral gap / mixing time per MCMC step), not in time. A
  classical local or uniform M-H step is O(n) to O(n^2) arithmetic, while each quantum step is a full circuit
  execution plus readout. The paper does not argue that the per-step overhead is covered.

7.3 Baselines -- what was and was NOT compared
- Compared: M-H local (single spin flip), M-H uniform (independent random), "mismatched quantum" control, the
  Gibbs/Glauber acceptance variants, lazy chains, Swendsen-Wang (2 variants), Wolff (2 variants), and Houdayer
  (R=2, single T) on illustrative instances.
- NOT compared: parallel tempering / replica exchange (explicitly excluded: "these steps are often wrapped in a
  parallel tempering scheme ... our quantum algorithm could also be wrapped in similar ways, although such
  generalizations are beyond the scope of this initial work. To allow a fair comparison, then, we use only two
  replicas at a single temperature T. (Note that this restriction to a single T precludes comparison with another
  cluster algorithm called isoenergetic cluster moves [18].)" [SI p.14]). Also not compared: isoenergetic cluster
  moves, population annealing, simulated annealing, tensor-network or exact-enumeration samplers, neural
  samplers, multi-spin/Kawasaki moves, or a classically simulated version of the same quantum proposal as a
  baseline. [not in P1]
- PT is framed as a future composite: "for instance, by running several local M-H chains in parallel at different
  temperatures and occasionally swapping them [18, 19, 45]. Our quantum algorithm may provide a potent new
  ingredient for such composite algorithms in the near term." [p.6]
- [INF] Classical simulability: P1 says the chain is "hard to mimic classically, provided it is classically hard
  to sample the measurement outcomes of U|s>" (p.2). It gives no hardness evidence for the specific
  low-depth 1D circuits used. 1D n<=10 circuits are trivially simulable classically.

7.4 Future directions stated
- Better choice of gamma, t ("a more targeted method of picking the parameters gamma and t could further
  accelerate convergence [46]"). [p.7]
- Other hardware, e.g. quantum annealers, "perhaps without needing to discretize the Hamiltonian dynamics". [p.7]
- Composite algorithms (PT, etc.). [p.6]
- Applications: simulated annealing, thermal averages, Boltzmann machines. [p.7]
- Alternative proposals: QPE-based (Fig. S33). Judged of "little practical appeal ... the only difference
  between this QPE method and the random-t method ... is the set of possible values from which t is drawn"
  [SI pp.36-37]. Also reverse annealing with a palindromic schedule (Eq. S47). [SI Sec. V C]
- Online implementation awaits dynamic-circuit infrastructure [36, 37]. [SI p.20]

=====================================================================================
8. HEURISTIC THEORY OF WHY IT WORKS
=====================================================================================
- Main-text intuition: with a dense H_mix and gamma << 1, perturbation theory shows that quantum proposals
  "exhibit a remarkable combination of features that suggest fast MCMC convergence: Like local proposals, their
  absolute energy change |DE| = |E(s') - E(s)| is typically small, meaning they are likely to be accepted even if
  DE > 0. However, like uniform or cluster proposals, s and s' are typically far in Hamming distance". Similar
  behaviour for H_mix = sum X_j and larger gamma is cited from refs [21-23] (Baldwin-Laumann; Smelyanskiy et al.).
  [p.3]
- SI Sec. V A (perturbative, interaction picture w.r.t. alpha H_prob):
  * Dyson to leading order: Pr(j->k) = gamma^2 t^2 sinc^2(t alpha DE / 2) |<k|H_mix|j>|^2 + O(gamma^3)
    [Eq. S35]. "The sinc-squared factor above acts as a bandpass filter for DE". With a dense H_mix (Grover-like,
    |<k|H_mix|j>| = 1) and t = 2 pi / (alpha eps), transitions occur mainly when |DE| <~ eps. [SI p.35]
  * Magnus/rotating-wave version: an effective Hamiltonian couples mainly near-degenerate states. [Eq. S36]
  * Long random t (t ~ U[0,t'], t' -> infinity): Pr(j->k) = sum_l |<k|l><l|j>|^2 (Eq. S37). With
    non-degenerate perturbation theory, Pr(j->k) = 2 (gamma/(alpha DE))^2 |<k|H_mix|j>|^2 + O(gamma^3) (Eq. S39),
    which is "the same DE^-2 scaling". The role of eigenvectors of H overlapping both |j> and |k> when
    E(j) ~ E(k). [SI p.36]
  * [INF] With H_mix = sum X_j, <k|H_mix|j> is nonzero only at Hamming distance 1. So the leading-order
    perturbative picture yields only LOCAL energy-conserving moves. The far-in-Hamming behaviour relies on the
    non-perturbative regime (gamma in [0.25, 0.6]). P1 says this: "(i) H_mix can be sparse ... quantum transitions
    can occur with high probability between states |j> and |k> even if <k|H_mix|j> = 0. (ii) Trivial |j> -> |j>
    'transitions' need not dominate ... (iii) The resulting dynamics can be hard to simulate classically. However,
    it is not clear a priori how the results of Refs. [49-51] translate into MCMC performance." [SI p.36]
  * Disclaimer: "We therefore view the perturbative calculations above, and Refs. [49-52], as providing
    motivation for our algorithm -- which we then evaluate empirically -- rather than any formal guarantee of
    performance." [SI p.36]
- Empirical mechanism evidence: Fig. 5c-d (Hamming distance spread like uniform; |DE| concentrated more than
  local in theory). Mismatched-quantum control: k = 0.76(1), about the same as uniform, so "the delta enhancement
  in our algorithm indeed arises from exploring the classical energy landscape E in quantum superposition to
  propose moves." [p.4]
- Why random (gamma, t) works: "some fraction of the moves will be particularly good, which proves sufficient for
  a quantum enhancement" [SI p.3]; "not every jump needs to be especially good for the algorithm to work well.
  (This is why picking gamma and t at random works well: good values arise often enough.)" [p.7]
