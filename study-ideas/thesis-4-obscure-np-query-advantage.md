# Thesis Idea 4 — Where Does Quantum Advantage Actually Survive? A systematic map, across five forgotten NP-complete problems, of where the provable quadratic query speedup beats the *best-known* classical algorithm and where it collapses to brute-force-only

**Tag: QUANTUM · thesis (qh / number-partitioning family) · effort: medium · QC: sim-first (query-count), optional hardware feasibility arm**

## The one-line contribution (this is the thesis)
> **A systematic, per-problem map of where the provable quantum query advantage survives the best-classical baseline and where it collapses to brute-force-only** — built on five old, obscure NP-complete problems nobody has done the classical-baseline homework on.

Everything below serves that sentence.

## Pitch
"Quantum advantage on an NP-complete problem" is usually an overclaim — but one form is a **theorem, not a benchmark**: Dürr–Høyer minimum-finding (on Grover) solves the optimization version of *any* NP-complete problem in Θ(2^{n/2}) cost-oracle calls, and by BBBV that quadratic speedup is **provably optimal in the query model** — unconditional, independent of P vs NP. The catch that turns it into a real research question: that quadratic speedup beats the *best known classical* algorithm **only when the best known classical algorithm is itself brute force**. For famous problems it isn't (number partitioning: classical meet-in-the-middle already runs Õ(2^{n/2}) → the advantage is *erased*). So the thesis is **not** "I verified quantum advantage" (an overclaim) and **not** "I ran them on a quantum computer and beat classical" (false on NISQ). It is a **map**: take five 1970s NP-complete problems with essentially zero quantum literature, do the classical-baseline homework nobody did, and determine for each whether the provably-optimal quadratic query advantage **survives** (best-known-classical is exhaustive → real advantage over the state of the art) or **collapses to brute-force-only** (a smarter classical algorithm already matches it). The mixed answer *is* the result.
**Paper strength score: 76/100** — the headline is a theorem you demonstrate up front (safe: "does advantage exist here?" is answered before any hardware), the obscurity gives near-sole ownership, and the honest survive/collapse map is more defensible and more novel than any uniform "advantage!" claim; docked because the mechanism (Grover/Dürr–Høyer) is textbook, so the entire contribution rides on the rigor of the per-problem best-classical analysis.

## Claim discipline (locked — the framing that keeps it bulletproof)
Four qualifiers attach to every advantage statement, no exceptions: **query model · over brute force · quadratic · not wall-clock.**

| CAN say (defensible) | CANNOT say (overclaim that sinks it) |
|---|---|
| "BBBV-optimal quadratic quantum **query** speedup over brute-force search, shown by oracle-count on all 5." (theorem, always true) | "I ran them on a quantum computer and found better-than-classical solutions." (NISQ ≠ advantage; QAOA likely **loses**) |
| "On the *k of 5* problems whose best known classical algorithm is exhaustive, this is a provable advantage over the **best known** method." (true after the homework) | "I systematically verified quantum advantage." (unqualified → reads as beating best classical everywhere → false) |
| "A map of **where** the advantage survives vs collapses to brute-force-only." (the contribution) | "quantum advantage" with no qualifier, or presenting a hardware run as the advantage proof |

The provable speedup lives in the **query model, demonstrated by counting oracle calls in simulation + fault-tolerant resource estimates** — you do NOT run Grover on a chip to beat brute force (it needs the oracle repeated ~2^{n/2} times at coherent depth; NISQ cannot). The hardware arm, if included, is a **feasibility/obstruction** result, a *different* claim from the advantage proof.

## How it becomes a study
**Research question:** For each of five obscure 1970s NP-complete problems, does the provably-optimal quadratic quantum query speedup (Dürr–Høyer/Grover) survive the best-known-classical baseline (best classical is still brute force → real advantage) or collapse to brute-force-only (a smarter classical algorithm already matches Θ(2^{n/2}))?
**Hypothesis:** Because these problems were proven complete in the 1970s and then abandoned, several still have **no** meet-in-the-middle / clever exponential algorithm, so for those the quadratic query advantage over best-known-classical is real and provable; others (e.g. any with a known 2^n dynamic program) will collapse to brute-force-only. (Falsifiable per problem, in *both* directions: finding a sub-brute-force classical algorithm collapses a "survives"; proving none is known upgrades a problem to "survives.")
**Method — three chapters, in dependency order:**
1. **Theorem chapter (advantage, in simulation).** For each problem, implement the cost oracle, run Dürr–Høyer / Grover-minimum in a simulator for small n, **count oracle calls**, fit the 2^{n/2} law against the 2^n brute-force count, and give a fault-tolerant resource estimate (qubits, T-count). This establishes the *provable* quadratic query speedup — identically for all five (it's the theorem).
2. **Baseline chapter (the map — this is the real work).** For each problem, survey and pin down the **best known classical exponential algorithm** and its exponent; classify each as **SURVIVES** (best-known-classical ≈ brute force → quantum beats best classical) or **COLLAPSES** (a better classical algorithm matches/beats 2^{n/2} → quantum beats only brute force). Output = the survive/collapse map + the exponent table behind it.
3. **Hardware chapter (feasibility, optional, honestly labeled).** Give each problem's QUBO/Ising map, run a fixed-depth QAOA arm on the existing number-partitioning swapnet codebase, and report approximation ratio vs a matched classical local baseline — presented as *feasibility + the Farhi–Gamarnik–Gutmann locality obstruction*, **never** as advantage.
**Baseline:** Classical brute force (always) AND the best known classical exponential algorithm per problem (the bar that decides survive vs collapse); for the hardware arm, a matched classical local/greedy heuristic.
**Metrics:** fitted oracle-call exponent vs n; fault-tolerant qubit/T-count; **per-problem best-known-classical exponent (the number the whole map turns on)**; survive/collapse verdict per problem; QAOA approximation ratio vs classical baseline (feasibility arm).
**Novel contribution:** The first systematic survive/collapse map of provable quantum **query** advantage across forgotten NP-complete problems — converting the vague "quantum can't beat classical on NP" into a precise, per-problem, theorem-backed ledger of exactly where a real edge exists and where classical already closed it.

## The five problems (each: definition · 1970s citation · QUBO map · expected survive/collapse)

**P1 — Betweenness (Total Ordering).** Given triples (a,b,c), order the set placing b between a and c for as many triples as possible. *Opatrný, SIAM J. Comput. 8(1):111–114, **1979** (G&J [MS1]).* QUBO: one-hot position vars x_{i,p}, per-triple degree-2 reward on the induced-order sign + permutation penalties; medium density. *Map:* likely **SURVIVES** — check for a sub-n!/sub-2^n ordering DP; APX-hard, frustrated random-triple hard regime. Best all-round shape; ordering encoding = modest codebase extension.

**P2 — Numerical Matching with Target Sums.** Pair one X- and one Y-element per target so each pair hits its target sum. *Garey & Johnson strong-NP-completeness, J. ACM 25(3):499–508, **1978** (G&J [SP17]; cite the JACM paper, not the appendix).* QUBO: assignment vars + per-target sum-to-target penalty (Σ s·x − B)² — **literally your partition penalty per target → maximal code reuse**; block-structured medium density. *Map:* **strongly** NP-complete (no pseudo-poly, hard at small integers → hardware-friendly couplings); check whether a meet-in-the-middle exists across the matching. Strongest "buildable + likely-survives" combo.

**P3 — Quadratic Congruences.** Does ∃ integer x, 0<x<c, with x² ≡ a (mod b)? *Manders & Adleman, J. Comput. Syst. Sci. 16(2):168–184, **1978** (G&J [AN1]).* QUBO: binary-expand x, penalty (x² − a − b·t)² — **quartic/HUBO, needs quadratization ancillas** (build risk); dense among the ~log c bits, large coefficients. *Map:* **highest collapse risk** — number theory is full of clever algorithms, so the best-classical check is most likely to demote it to brute-force-only. High novelty; keep expressly to demonstrate an honest *collapse* case.

**P4 — Kernel of a Digraph.** Find an independent, absorbing vertex set. *Chvátal, CRM-300 tech report, Univ. Montréal, **1973** (strengthened: Fraenkel, Discrete Appl. Math. 3(4):257–262, 1981 — planar, in/out-degree ≤2).* QUBO: membership vars, independence penalty on intra-set arcs, per-vertex absorption penalty; **sparse/local → best hardware fit.** *Map:* cleanest obscurity (zero quantum work); feasibility problem → recast as constraint-violation minimization; check best-known classical for kernel existence. *Cite carefully — tech report, not a journal.*

**P5 — Minimum Linear Arrangement (MinLA).** Place vertices on a line minimizing Σ_{(i,j)∈E}|pos(i)−pos(j)|. *Garey, Johnson & Stockmeyer, Theor. Comput. Sci. 1(3):237–267, **1976**.* QUBO: one-hot positions (shared with P1), tabulated |p−q| edge couplings; graph-structured medium density (build P1+P5 together). *Map:* likely **COLLAPSES** — a known 2^n·poly Held–Karp-style DP means the honest verdict is probably advantage-over-brute-force-only. Deliberately included as the clean *collapse* contrast that proves the map has teeth.

*(Reserve/validation: **3-Partition** — Garey & Johnson, SIAM J. Comput. 4(4):397–411, 1975 — strongly NP-complete, trivial reuse, but NOT obscure (2-way partitioning is well-quantized) → warm-up/validation arm only. **Set Basis** (Stockmeyer, IBM RC-5431, 1975), **Cutwidth** (Gavril, CISS 1977) — more obscure, costlier maps, reserves.)*

A map with **both** survivors (P1/P2 likely) and collapses (P3/P5 likely) is the honest, defensible outcome — a uniform "all survive" result would itself be a red flag.

## Bull case / Bear case / Likely outcome / Value if null
**Bull:** The headline is a theorem provable and demonstrable up front (query-count + BBBV optimality), so "does advantage exist here?" is settled with certainty before any hardware — and you own five problems nobody has mapped. Directly satisfies "known possible right away," and the survive/collapse framing is overclaim-proof.
**Bear:** The mechanism is textbook Grover, so credibility rides entirely on the best-classical analysis being genuinely rigorous; if a problem's classical literature is thin or ambiguous, its verdict may be "unknown" rather than clean survive/collapse.
**Likely outcome:** 2–3 problems map to **survives** (real quadratic advantage over best-known-classical), 2 to **collapses** (brute-force-only), the hardware arm reproduces the known obstruction — a clean, honest, well-scoped thesis whose figure is the map itself.
**Value if null:** If *every* candidate collapses (all have clever classical algorithms), the deliverable — a resource-costed ledger showing the provable quadratic query speedup is matched classically even on problems nobody had checked — is itself a citable mapping/negative result and still answers the question.
